What an interior designer costs in Israel, and how to compare proposals

By SELÈ STUDIO · Hiring a designer · Published September 28, 2026

“How much does an interior designer cost?” is almost always the first question, and rightly so. Before you start dreaming about a stone island or an oak library, you want to know what you are committing to. The honest answer is that there is no single price. An interior designer’s fee comes from two things: the pricing model, and the scope of work that is actually defined. That means how many spaces, how much joinery and how many drawings, and whether the design ends with a set of plans or continues with renovation support until you move in.

You won’t find a price list here. You’ll find something more useful: an explanation of the four common pricing models, the factors that push the fee up or down, and a way to compare proposals that look different. You’ll also find what it costs, sometimes far more, to skip design altogether. With these tools you compare proposals properly, not just numbers.

Four common pricing models in interior design

Interior designers usually work with one of four models, or a combination of them. Each has its own logic, and each comes with a question worth asking before you sign.

Per square meter

The fee is set by the size of the home. The advantage is obvious: it is easy to understand and easy to compare. The drawback is less obvious. A square meter of kitchen or bathroom takes far more design work than a square meter of bedroom, and a small apartment with a complex kitchen and a lot of joinery can need more work than a large, simple one.

Ask: which areas are counted (gross or net, balconies, storage), and exactly what is included in the rate.

Fixed fee for the project

One sum, agreed in advance, for a defined scope. It gives you the most certainty, provided the scope really is defined: which spaces, which drawings, how many alternatives and how many site visits.

Ask: what happens if you add a room or change direction halfway, and how out-of-scope changes are priced.

Hourly or per consultation

This suits focused work, such as advice before buying an apartment, choosing materials and colors, reviewing the plan you received from the developer, or solving a single space. You pay for the time actually spent, which makes it a good way to start small.

Ask: how many hours the task is expected to take, and how they are logged and reported.

Percentage of the build budget

The fee is calculated as a percentage of construction costs. This model is most common on large projects, and it ties the fee to the overall size of the project. The drawback: when the budget rises, the fee rises too, even if the design work has not changed.

Ask: what the percentage is calculated on: only the shell and finishing work, or also furniture, appliances and lighting.

In practice: combinations and phased pricing

Many proposals are divided into phases, and that is also the practical answer to what an interior design service includes. The common phases are design (layout and drawings), detailing and material selection, supervision during the build, and sometimes styling. This lets you decide how much support you need and see what you are paying for at each stage.

What affects the cost of an interior designer?

Even under the same pricing model, two projects of the same size can be very different. These are the factors that set the actual scope of work.

The number of spaces, and what happens in them

Kitchens and bathrooms are the densest spaces to design. Plumbing, electrics, stone, flooring and cladding, lighting and joinery all meet within a few square meters. A living room or bedroom is simpler, unless it has a joinery wall, a dropped ceiling or concealed lighting. So the question is not only how many rooms there are, but how many of them are complex.

The amount of joinery

Every custom wardrobe, bookcase or TV wall needs its own drawings: fronts, internal layout, handles, where it meets the stone and the lighting, and exact dimensions for the joiner. A home with wall-to-wall, floor-to-ceiling joinery needs far more drawing and detailing than one furnished mostly with ready-made pieces. Planning custom joinery is a subject of its own, and worth understanding before you compare proposals.

The number of drawings, alternatives and revision rounds

A full drawing set can include a measured survey, demolition and construction plans, a furniture layout, electrical and data, lighting, plumbing, flooring and cladding, ceilings and joinery details. Some proposals include one option for the layout, and others include several. Some include 3D renders of every space, and others cover only the kitchen. Each of these is work, and so each is part of the price.

The scope of on-site support

A design that ends with handing over drawings is very different from support on site, which includes meetings with contractors and suppliers, site visits, and checking dimensions before stone and joinery are ordered. This is usually the component that varies most between proposals, which is why it matters most to define it in numbers: how many visits, at which stages, and the cost of an extra visit.

Materials, furniture and styling

Showroom visits for stone, tiles and lighting, purchase lists, and choosing furniture and textiles at the end can be part of the service, or they can stay with you. It is worth knowing in advance which of them are included.

The starting point

A new-build apartment, where changes can be planned before the walls are closed, is different from an older apartment where surprises wait behind the plaster. Timing matters too: design under time pressure needs more hours in less time.

How to compare interior designer proposals

Before comparing totals, put the proposals on the same footing. The simple way: one table, a row for each item and a column for each proposal. An item missing from one proposal is not a “zero”. It is a cost that will arrive later, or work that won’t get done. These are the rows worth having in the table:

  • Scope: which spaces are included, and which are not.
  • Deliverables: the list of drawings you will receive, each named.
  • Alternatives and revisions: how many layout options, and how many rounds of changes at each stage.
  • Renders: whether they are included, and for which spaces.
  • Materials and procurement: whether there are selection visits, purchase lists and liaison with suppliers.
  • Supervision: the number of site visits and meetings, and the cost of an extra visit.
  • Exclusions: outside consultants (structural, HVAC, electrical) and permits, where required. This is also where it helps to know when you also need an architect.
  • Out-of-scope changes: how they are priced.
  • Payment schedule: payments tied to phases and milestones, not everything upfront.
  • VAT: whether the sum includes VAT.

Two proposals that look far apart in price sometimes turn out to be very close once you compare them line by line, and sometimes the reverse. The questions for the meeting itself, and what to listen for in the answers, are in our guide to choosing an interior designer.

What does it cost to skip design?

You see an interior designer’s fee in the proposal. You see the cost of no design only in hindsight, and usually on someone else’s invoice:

  • Fixes after the wall is closed. A socket stuck behind a cabinet, a drain that doesn’t suit the vanity or a forgotten lighting point is discovered after the cladding is on the wall. Fixing it at that stage means taking things apart, and paying twice for the same work.
  • Orders that don’t fit each other. Stone cut before the joinery was measured, a sofa that won’t go through the door, flooring that doesn’t finish cleanly against the panels. Each one is a repeat order.
  • Extras on the contractor’s bill. Without drawings and a bill of quantities, each contractor is effectively pricing a different apartment. Whatever wasn’t defined up front arrives later as an “extra”, and the difference shows up in the final bill.
  • Decisions under pressure. When every choice is made on the day the supplier needs an answer, it is easy to pay more and get less.

The design fee is part of the renovation budget, but it is the part that decides how the rest behaves. Careful design does not prevent every surprise. It reduces their number, and makes sure someone knows how to solve them.

How to get a real number for your project

An accurate number starts with a conversation. It helps to come with:

  • a plan of the apartment or house, if you have one, and photos of how it looks now;
  • a list of the spaces you want to change, and what matters to you in each;
  • a budget range for the build, since even a rough estimate helps match the scope of design;
  • a timeline: when you get the keys, and when you want to move in;
  • inspiration: images, materials or homes you have loved.

From that conversation a scope can be defined. Only a defined scope produces a proposal that fits your home, rather than an “average apartment”.

Frequently asked questions

Does a per-square-meter price cover the whole apartment?

It depends on the designer and the proposal. Some calculate on gross area, some on net, and some price balconies and service areas differently. Before comparing two per-meter proposals, ask exactly which areas are counted and which drawings and services are included in the rate.

Should I choose the cheapest proposal?

Only after comparing what it includes. A cheaper proposal sometimes saves on exactly what is expensive to add later: site visits, renders or revision rounds that will be priced separately. And sometimes the more expensive proposal includes services you don’t need. Compare line by line, and only then the totals.

Does the designer’s fee include materials, furniture and contractors?

Usually not. The fee pays for design and supervision, and construction, materials and furniture are separate costs. Under the percentage model they serve only as the basis for the calculation. If any of them is included, it should be stated explicitly in the proposal.

Why do proposals from different designers vary so much?

Often because they are pricing different work. Differences in the number of drawings, the alternatives, the renders and above all the amount of on-site supervision create large gaps. Ask for detail on any item that isn’t clear.

What is the difference between design only and design with supervision?

With design only, you receive a set of drawings and carry them out with your contractors yourself. With supervision, the designer is also on site: she meets contractors and suppliers, checks dimensions before orders and answers the questions that arise during the build. The difference shows in the price and in your peace of mind.
