Choosing kitchen countertop stone: the material, finish and details that shape the look

By SELÈ STUDIO · Materials & details · Published September 28, 2026

Marble, quartz or porcelain? In Israel almost any kitchen worktop gets called “shayish”, marble, but the word covers very different families of material: stone quarried from a mountain, engineered stone compressed in a factory, and a ceramic slab fired at high temperature. They differ in how they look, how they react to lemon, wine and a hot pan, and what they ask of you over the years.

This guide helps you choose kitchen countertop stone by how you actually live in your kitchen, not by a beautiful sample in a showroom. We go through each family: look, porosity and staining, heat and scratches, maintenance, edges and thickness. Then we get to the details that decide whether a kitchen looks calm and precise: the veining on the island, the finish, splashback continuity and the sink. You won’t find prices here. They vary between suppliers, series and fabrication, so they are only compared on the basis of a defined detail: material, thickness, edges and cut-outs. And if you are at the start of the process, all of these choices come together in kitchen design and planning.

Before the sample: four questions that matter more than any catalog

  • How do you cook? Someone who puts pans straight off the flame, chops a lot and squeezes lemons every morning needs a different surface from someone whose kitchen is mostly for coffee and hosting.
  • How much patina are you willing to accept? Some people see a faint mark on marble as a memory; others find it bothersome. An honest answer to this question narrows the choice more than any technical figure.
  • What does the light do in your kitchen? Low sun picks out every scratch on a polished surface, and concealed light falling at an angle brings out texture.
  • Where else will the stone appear? Island, splashback, niche, sill or a sink in the same material. The more places the stone appears, the more slab size and vein direction become part of the design.

Natural stone for the kitchen: marble, quartzite and granite

Look. With natural stone every slab is one of a kind. The veining runs through the full thickness, so the edge, the sink cut-out and the side of the island all look genuine. Marble brings softness and glow, quartzite brings dramatic veining with high hardness, and granite is more speckled and less common in minimalist kitchens. Travertine and limestone belong to the same family, with higher porosity; we cover them in our travertine guide.

Porosity and staining. Marble is recrystallized limestone, and it reacts to acid. Lemon, vinegar, wine and tomato sauce leave etching on it: a dull mark on a polished surface. Sealing reduces absorption of oil and color but does not stop the reaction to acid. True quartzite and granite are more resistant, but not everything sold as quartzite is quartzite: some slabs are dolomite or hard marble. A drop of lemon and a scratch with the rim of a glass on a sample will save a disappointment.

Heat and scratches. Natural stone doesn’t burn, but a sharp, local change in temperature can crack it. Marble is relatively soft and scratches from knives and stoneware; quartzite and granite are far harder.

Maintenance. Periodic sealing as the supplier directs, cleaning with a neutral product with no acid and no abrasive powder, and wiping up spills quickly. Accept the patina and you get a surface that ages beautifully.

Edges and thickness. Natural stone can be worked to almost any profile: a straight, crisp edge, a slightly rounded one, or a miter, a 45-degree joint that gives the look of a thick block. Common thicknesses are 2 and 3 cm.

Quartz for the kitchen: engineered stone

Look. Engineered quartz is made of crushed quartz bound with resin and pigments. The result is uniform and predictable, and the sample closely matches the slab you’ll receive. Newer series imitate marble veining, and in some the vein runs through the thickness too, but a trained eye picks up the repetition.

Porosity and staining. It is almost non-porous, so it needs no sealing and resists stains and acids very well. That is its great advantage in a busy kitchen.

Heat and scratches. Its weak point is the resin. A hot pan straight off the flame can leave a mark, a color change or a crack, so a trivet is a must. It resists everyday scratches, but it isn’t suited to outdoor use, since UV can change its color.

Maintenance. Water, mild soap and a cloth, with no harsh or solvent-based products.

Edges and thickness. Common thicknesses are 1.2, 2 and 3 cm, with a straight, rounded or mitered edge.

A word on safety. Awareness has grown in recent years of silicosis, a lung disease affecting workers who cut and polish high-silica engineered stone. Australia banned its use from 2024, and manufacturers are launching low-silica series. The risk lies in the dust created during fabrication, not in the installed worktop, and it is worth asking your supplier about working conditions.

Porcelain kitchen worktops

Look. Porcelain and its close relative, sintered stone, are ceramic slabs fired at very high temperature. They come in large, thin formats in the tones of stone, concrete and metal, and can be remarkably convincing. In most products, though, the vein is printed on the surface only, and an edge, a sink cut-out or a chip reveals a body of a different color. That is why porcelain is designed around its edges, usually mitered, and why through-body series are preferred wherever cuts will be visible.

Porosity and staining. Near-zero absorption. It resists stains and acids very well.

Heat and scratches. Highly resistant to heat and UV, and suitable for outdoor kitchens too. It is very hard to scratch, so hard that knives blunt on it. Its weakness is at the edges and corners: a knock from a heavy pan can chip it, and repairing a chip in porcelain is harder than in other materials.

Maintenance. The simplest of all: water and soap, no sealing.

Edges and thickness. Thin formats of 6, 12 and 20 mm, with few profile options. The thin edge makes a crisp, contemporary line, and a miter gives the look of a block. Porcelain needs the right equipment and experience to fabricate, so choose a fabricator who has done it many times.

A stone island: veining, bookmatching and miters

A stone island that reads as one block isn’t a matter of the model you pick; it’s a matter of the cutting plan. Where the stone drops from the worktop to the sides, the pieces are cut in sequence from the same slab so the vein carries on over the miter line. This is marked out with the fabricator on a photo of the actual slab, and with natural stone you also choose the slab in the yard.

Bookmatching means opening two consecutive slabs from the same block like the pages of a book, so the veining forms a mirror image. It works beautifully on a wide island front or a splashback wall, and it needs consecutive slabs. In quartz and porcelain it’s possible only in series made for it.

Three more decisions are worth making early: where the joint goes if the island is longer than the slab, how much stone to leave between the cooktop and sink cut-outs and the edge of the worktop, and how to support a seating overhang.

Polished, honed or leathered?

  • Polished: gives depth to color and vein, reflects light and wipes clean easily. On marble it shows etching and scratches.
  • Honed (matte): soft and quiet, and it hides light etching, but it is more open to stains and needs careful sealing. On quartz and porcelain with a matte finish, gray marks from the bottoms of pans can appear; they clean off.
  • Leathered (brushed): a subtle texture that hides fingerprints and water spots, feels good to the touch and comes alive under concealed light. It needs slightly more thorough cleaning.

See every sample in your own light, by day and in the evening, before you decide.

Splashback continuity: when the stone goes up the wall

When the worktop continues up the wall in the same stone, ideally as a single slab, the kitchen calms down: fewer grout lines to clean, fewer lines for the eye. In a niche, the back, sides and shelf can all be clad in the same material. Sockets, a wall-mounted tap and rails are positioned before cutting, because a hole in a large slab can’t be moved. Concealed light at 2700–⁠3000K over a veined splashback adds depth, as long as the strip itself stays out of sight; we go into this in our guide to planning home lighting. Thin, light porcelain is especially well suited to cladding walls in large formats.

Undermount or integrated sink?

  • An undermount sink sits beneath the cut-out with a polished inner edge, so crumbs can be swept straight in. With printed porcelain, make sure the inner edge won’t reveal a body of a different tone.
  • An integrated sink is built from the same stone, with mitered joints and a base that slopes to the drain. It reads as a continuation of the worktop, with no rim and no seam, and it demands a particularly skilled fabricator.
  • A top-mount sink is simple to install and replace, but its rim collects dirt and breaks the line.

Drainer grooves carved beside the sink are practical, but on polished marble they are also where water stands. That’s a design decision, not a default.

How to choose in practice: from sample to slab

  1. Test a sample at home: lemon, oil, red wine and coffee, covered overnight; a hot mug; a scratch with a key. What happens to the sample will happen to the worktop.
  2. See the full slab. With natural stone this is essential, and it’s worth marking which part goes on the island front.
  3. Template only after the cabinets are installed, so the worktop is cut to reality rather than to the drawing.
  4. Ask the fabricator about joints, edges, cut-outs, delivery and protecting the slab during the renovation.
  5. Get care instructions in writing for the material and finish you chose.

Frequently asked questions

Which is better for a kitchen, marble or quartz?

It depends on use and on your patience for patina. Natural marble is unique and ages beautifully, but it reacts to acid and needs sealing. Quartz is uniform, non-porous and very stain-resistant, but sensitive to direct heat. In a kitchen that cooks a lot, with no wish to look after the surface, quartz or porcelain will be calmer.

Can I put a hot pan directly on a stone worktop?

On porcelain, and on most hard natural stones, usually yes, although a sharp change in temperature can crack any stone. On engineered quartz, no: its resin is sensitive to heat. The safest habit with any material is a trivet.

Does a porcelain worktop break easily?

The slab itself is very hard and highly resistant to scratches and heat. The sensitive points are the edges and corners, which can chip from a sharp knock. That’s why edges are mitered or slightly rounded, an experienced fabricator is chosen, and through-body series are preferred.

Do quartz or porcelain worktops need sealing?

No. Both absorb almost nothing. Sealing is needed for natural stone, at intervals that depend on the stone, the finish and the sealer. A simple test: if a drop of water left for a few minutes darkens the stone, it’s time to reseal.

What is bookmatching, and which materials allow it?

Two consecutive slabs from the same block, opened like the pages of a book so the veining forms a mirror image. It comes naturally mainly with natural stone, and requires choosing consecutive slabs in the yard. In quartz and porcelain it’s possible only in special series designed for it.

Which finish is most practical in a kitchen?

No finish is perfect. Polished wipes clean easily but shows scratches and etching on marble. Honed is quieter but more open to stains. Leathered hides fingerprints and water spots. The best way to decide is to look at a sample in your own kitchen’s light.
