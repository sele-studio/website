Custom joinery: how to design wardrobes, bookcases and wood walls that belong to the home

By SELÈ STUDIO · Materials & details · Published September 28, 2026

A wardrobe, a bookcase or a wood-paneled wall is almost the only thing in a home you don’t buy ready-made; you design it. It is built to the dimensions of one particular wall, it meets the floor, the ceiling, the wiring and the light, and it stays there for years. That makes custom joinery one of the decisions that most shapes how a home looks and feels. Planned well, it disappears into the architecture of the room. Planned badly, you see every filler strip, every handle and every cable.

This guide walks through the decisions that determine the quality of joinery, from the material to the smallest detail: veneer, solid wood or lacquer; light or dark oak; proportions and shadow gaps; handles, ventilation and lighting; and how all of it reaches the joiner as a drawing that can actually be built. It is written for anyone planning a renovation or a new apartment who wants to arrive at the meeting with the joiner knowing what to ask. Remember that joinery is part of designing the whole home, not a separate project that starts after everything else has been decided.

Why joinery is designed, not bought

Ready-made cabinets are built to average dimensions, and a real wall is never average. The ceiling isn’t perfectly level, there is a beam, a window close to the corner and a socket in the middle of the wall. Custom joinery solves all of that, but only if someone has decided in advance what it needs to do. Three questions come before any choice of material:

  • What goes inside. Books, towels, coats, a fridge, an air-conditioning unit, a router. The internal layout follows from this list, and it sets the depth, the shelf heights and the number of drawers.
  • How it is used. A cabinet opened ten times a day needs different hardware and a different grip from one opened once a season.
  • What role it plays in the room. A quiet backdrop, a wall that anchors the living room, or a piece that draws the eye. A living-room bookcase and a bedroom wardrobe can be made from the same wood and still play entirely different roles.

Veneer or solid wood? And what about lacquer

Veneer

Veneer is a thin slice of real wood, usually under a millimeter thick, bonded to a stable board. You get the look and feel of natural wood without the movement of solid timber, which is why it is the usual choice for large fronts, wall paneling and tall cabinets. An advantage people rarely mention: the grain can be laid out in sequence across several doors, so a whole wall reads as one surface. The drawback is that veneer can only be refinished to a limited extent, and deep damage is hard to repair.

Solid wood

Solid wood is alive: it expands and contracts with humidity, and wide panels of it can warp. In built-in joinery it is therefore used mainly where it excels: edges, handles, thick shelves and surfaces you touch. It can be sanded and refinished for years, and it has a depth no imitation matches.

Lacquer

A painted front can take any color, matte or gloss, and gives a quiet, monolithic look. It is especially suited to fronts you want to dissolve into the wall. On the other hand, it is more vulnerable to knocks on the corners, and dark or glossy lacquer shows fingerprints.

And laminate?

Laminate boards (HPL, often called formica in Israel) are durable and more affordable, and some decors imitate wood well. Up close they lack the depth of real grain. A common solution is a mix: laminate for cabinet interiors and hidden parts, veneer or lacquer for the fronts you see.

Light or dark oak, and when to choose walnut

Oak is one of the most common woods in designed joinery, thanks to an even, calm grain that sits well with almost any stone. The tone is the big decision:

  • Light oak reflects light, opens up the room and creates a calm backdrop. It suits shared living spaces and apartments with limited daylight, and works beautifully beside warm-gray stone and pale plaster, as in the bookcase of the light-oak living room.
  • Dark oak creates depth, a frame and a sense of quiet. It works well as a single wall or as one mass of cabinetry, beside light fronts and a pale stone worktop. In a dark room, or when every wall is dark, it can feel heavy, and dust shows more on its horizontal surfaces.
  • Walnut is naturally dark, warmer than oak, with a lively, pronounced grain. Because of its strong character it is best in measured doses: drawers, a headboard wall, one cabinet that holds the eye.

Two practical tips. First, ask for a sample of the final finish, not just of the wood, because oil or lacquer changes the tone. Second, look at it in the room itself, in daylight and under warm evening light. Wood is a natural material: its tone varies from one production batch to the next, and also changes over time and with sun exposure. So the sample should come from the same batch of material the joinery will be made from.

The details that make the difference

An oak bookcase: rhythm, not just shelves

A wall of joinery reads first as a rhythm of lines. Doors of similar width, one top line that carries from room to room, and a considered meeting with windows, flooring and a stone base all create calm. In a bookcase, vertical dividers at regular intervals and shelves at varying heights give a lively rhythm without losing order. A continuous base beneath the bookcase, in stone or wood, ties it to the whole wall.

Floor-to-ceiling wardrobes

A wardrobe that reaches the ceiling reads as a wall rather than a piece of furniture, and leaves no dust shelf on top. Because ceilings are never perfectly level, a filler strip or a top shadow gap is designed to absorb the difference. Very tall doors tend to warp over the years, so they are given a door straightener, or split into two doors on a line that aligns with something else in the room. The top section, out of easy reach, is for what you use once a season.

Shadow gaps

A shadow gap is a narrow recessed space between two surfaces: between panels, between the joinery and the wall or ceiling, or at the base. It hides small inaccuracies and separates materials without a trim or frame. A recessed plinth at the bottom makes the body of the joinery appear to float. On a wood wall, thin vertical gaps between the panels are what turn cladding into a designed wall.

Integrated handles

The handle is the detail you touch most. The main options are a recessed profile running along the front, a routed grip at the edge of the door, a slim solid-wood pull, or push-to-open. Push-to-open looks clean, but it is less comfortable for heavy drawers and doors in constant use, and on dark lacquer it leaves fingerprints where you press. For a cabinet used every day, a recessed profile is usually the right balance between cleanliness and comfort.

Ventilation

A fridge, an oven or an air-conditioning unit inside joinery needs airflow according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and a concealed air-conditioning unit also needs service access. When this isn’t planned in advance, the solution arrives at the end as a hole or a mesh that ignores the lines. When it is planned, a linear grille sits exactly in the top of the cabinet or on the line of a gap, and becomes part of the joinery’s design language. In the kitchen, where joinery meets water, heat and stone, this coordination is especially critical, which is why it is central to kitchen planning.

Integrated lighting

An LED strip inside joinery sits in a channel that is cut into the wood during manufacture, so its place is set in the drawings, not on site. Its position sets the effect: at the front of a shelf the strip lights what sits on it, at the back it washes the wall and creates depth. In a bookcase with dividers, a vertical strip along each divider lights every compartment evenly, without the shelves casting shadows on each other. We cover the technical side (diffuser, power supply, light color) and the layers of light across the whole home in our guide to home lighting design.

A wooden TV wall

A TV wall is joinery and an electrical system at the same time. Before tone and material, plan a socket and a data point behind the screen, and a wide conduit from the screen to the media cabinet so cables disappear and can be replaced in the future. You also need ventilation for the set-top box and player, and reinforcement in the wall or panel to carry the bracket. A screen recessed into a niche or set into paneling, on one line with the bookcase and the base, reads as part of the wall rather than a device hung on it.

How the drawings reach the joiner

A good joiner builds what the drawing says. The more precise the drawing, the fewer decisions are left for the site. A complete set of joinery drawings usually includes:

  1. Plans and elevations of every wall, with dimensions, door divisions and opening directions.
  2. Sections and details wherever the joinery meets stone, ceiling, floor or lighting.
  3. A materials and finishes schedule with approved samples, and a hardware list: hinges, runners, mechanisms and handles.
  4. Electrical coordination: positions of sockets, LED strips, power supplies and sensors, so the electrician prepares them in time.
  5. A render, when you want to see the rhythm and the tones before deciding.

From there, the usual sequence is this: the joiner measures on site once the walls, floor and ceiling are finished, prepares shop drawings, and the designer checks them against the design before any cutting begins. Stone worktops are usually templated only after the cabinet carcasses are installed. This order prevents most surprises. The amount of joinery in a home is also one of the factors that shape the cost of design, so it is worth defining in the very first conversation.

Good joinery doesn’t shout. It holds what it needs to, opens easily, and leaves the room clean, precise and right.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between veneer and solid wood in joinery?

Veneer is a thin layer of real wood bonded to a stable board, so it doesn’t move with humidity and suits large fronts and wall paneling. Solid wood is wood all the way through; it can be sanded and refinished, but it expands and contracts, so it is better suited to edges, handles, shelves and work surfaces.

Are floor-to-ceiling wardrobes worth it?

In most cases, yes. A wardrobe to the ceiling reads as part of the wall, uses the full height and collects no dust on top. To keep it clean-looking, plan a filler strip or top shadow gap that hides an uneven ceiling, and decide in advance what will be stored in the top section.

Light or dark oak for an apartment with little daylight?

Light oak reflects more light and feels more spacious, so it is the safe choice in a dark room. Dark oak can work there too, but in measured doses: one wall or one block of cabinets beside light fronts, walls and surfaces. Check a finish sample in the room itself, by day and in the evening.

Can I order joinery directly from a joiner, without a designer?

You can, and many joiners also offer design of the unit itself. The difference is the point of view: a designer plans the joinery together with the room, the lighting, the wiring, the stone and the lines of the rest of the home. When a home has a lot of joinery, or the joinery is complex, that coordination is usually what separates a good cabinet from joinery that looks like part of the home.

When in a renovation should joinery be designed?

Early, before the electrical work. Sockets, LED strips, ventilation and TV-wall reinforcement must be ready before walls and ceilings are closed. The joiner’s final measurement is usually taken only once the walls, floor and ceiling are finished.
