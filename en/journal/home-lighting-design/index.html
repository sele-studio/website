Home lighting design: light in layers, warm and precise

By SELÈ STUDIO · Materials & details · Published September 28, 2026

Light is the one material in a home you cannot touch, and it changes everything else. The same stone worktop looks flat under a spotlight aimed straight down at it, and gains depth when soft light slides across it from the side. The same bedroom feels like an office under a ceiling full of downlights, and like a place to rest when the light drops low, to the height of the bed. So home lighting design is not choosing fittings from a catalog. It is deciding what you see, when, and in what mood.

This guide covers the three layers of light, color temperature and color rendering (CRI), concealed lighting in niches and behind mirrors, and how light meets stone and wood. It also covers the most practical question of all: when to plan all of this relative to the electrician and the carpenter. At the end you’ll find short notes for each room.

What are the layers of lighting?

A well-lit room never relies on a single source. It is built from three layers, each answering a different need and each controlled separately.

Ambient light: the quiet base

This is the light that lets you move through a space with confidence. In most homes it comes from recessed ceiling fittings, or from indirect light aimed at the ceiling and walls. The aim is even, soft light, without hot spots or glare. A deep-recessed downlight, with its source set well back, lights the room without the eye catching a bare point of light.

Task light: light where the work happens

Above the kitchen worktop, at the mirror, beside the armchair where you read. Task light is placed so that whoever is standing or sitting there doesn’t cast a shadow over the work surface. That’s why, in a kitchen, a strip under the wall cabinets beats a ceiling spot that sits behind your back.

Accent and mood light: what gives a room depth

A lit niche, a stone wall with light sliding across it, a low lamp in a corner. This layer is barely needed for function, but it’s the one that turns a room into a home. In the evening, when the ambient light goes down, it stays on.

The rule that ties all three together: each layer on its own circuit, ideally dimmable. That way the same room can be bright and practical at midday, and soft and quiet at night.

Color temperature: why 2700K feels like home

Color temperature is measured in kelvin (K). The lower the number, the warmer and more amber the light. Warm 2700K light is close to an old incandescent bulb and to early evening, which is why it’s the common choice for living rooms and bedrooms. 3000K is still warm, slightly crisper, and usually suits kitchens and bathrooms. 4000K is already neutral, and in living spaces it tends to feel like an office.

More important than the number itself is consistency. In an open-plan space, where the kitchen, dining area and living room are seen together, choose one temperature, or at most a small difference, for example 2700K in the living room and 3000K in the kitchen. Two temperatures that are too far apart in one view look patchy, even when each fitting on its own looks right.

Color rendering (CRI): light that shows a material as it is

This index describes how true to life colors look under a light source. Under a low-CRI source, oak loses its warmth, pale stone turns grayish and skin looks pallid. For a home built from natural materials, ask for a CRI of 90 or above, especially in the kitchen, the bathroom and anywhere light falls on stone or wood. The gap between a good source and an average one doesn’t show in a catalog. It shows on the wall straight away.

Concealed lighting: you see the light, not the source

Concealed lighting is an LED strip hidden in a profile, in a niche, under a shelf or behind a mirror, so the eye takes in the glow and not the lamp. Planned well, it looks as though the material itself is giving off light.

Backlit niche lighting in the kitchen

In the niche pictured, the strip is hidden in the niche head and washes the stone splashback from top to bottom. The veining stands out and the worktop gets clean light. On its own circuit, a niche like this can stay lit even when the rest of the kitchen is off. For this to work, the channel for the profile is cut into the joinery or the stone at the drawing stage. A strip stuck on afterwards doesn’t give the same line of light. You can see the full project in the stone and oak kitchen.

Backlit mirrors

A mirror lit from behind looks detached from the wall, and the light escaping around it brings out the texture of the stone behind it. But backlighting alone isn’t enough for shaving or make-up. It is a mood layer, and it needs face light beside it, from the front or the sides. In the travertine bathroom, two tall, slim mirrors are lit from behind. Recessed downlights show in the ceiling reflected in them, and they are the ambient layer that completes the mirrors.

The details that make the difference

  • A continuous line, no dots. A strip where you can see individual LED points reflects in polished stone and glass as a row of dots. A continuous-emission strip, or a profile with a diffuser, gives one soft line of light.
  • Hidden from every angle. Check the sightline standing, seated and from the doorway. A strip that’s hidden from someone standing can be exposed to someone sitting on the sofa.
  • Access to the driver. Every strip has a power supply (driver) that can run warm and may need replacing. It needs a ventilated, accessible spot, not one behind a bonded stone panel.
  • Matched dimming. The driver and the dimmer have to be compatible, or the light flickers or hums at low levels.

How to light stone and wood

Stone and wood are textured materials, and light from the right angle is what reveals that texture. Light that grazes along a surface, from above or below at a steep angle, brings out veining, the pores of travertine and the grain of wood. Light that hits a surface head-on flattens it.

In the picture, a light line is tucked under the base of the wood paneling and falls onto the stone plinth below. The two materials meet in a line of light, and the whole wall gains depth. A few principles:

  • The stone’s finish changes the lighting. Polished stone throws back every spotlight as a glaring point. On a honed or brushed finish, light is absorbed softly. Over a polished surface, prefer diffuse light to focused spots.
  • Dark wood absorbs light. A dark oak or walnut wall needs more light than you’d expect, or it reads as a hole. Light wood bounces light back and softens the whole room.
  • Warm light flatters both. Wood and natural stone respond well to 2700K–3000K with high color rendering. Cool light turns them gray.

We cover choosing the materials themselves in how to choose kitchen stone and our travertine guide.

When to plan the lighting: before the electrician, not after

Most lighting mistakes don’t come from choosing the wrong fitting. They come from timing. When lighting is decided only after the walls are closed, there’s no room left for a profile, a driver or a power point in the right place. In a renovation, the right order usually looks like this:

  1. Layout plan. First you know where the bed, the island, the sofa and the dining table go. Without that, there’s nothing to aim the light at.
  2. Lighting plan. Layers, fittings, circuits and switches are marked on the plan, together with the depth of any dropped ceilings.
  3. Joinery and stone drawings. Channels for light strips, space for drivers and power points inside the cabinets go into the drawings before they go to fabrication.
  4. Marking on site. Before the electrician starts, walk the wall and ceiling points together. It’s the last moment when a correction is a matter of a marker pen.
  5. Check before closing. Before plaster and plasterboard go up, confirm every point is in place and the cables for the strips reach the right spot.
  6. Installation and aiming. After paint and joinery, fittings are installed, spots are aimed and dimming is tested, in the evening and not just in daylight.

The electrical work itself is always done by a licensed electrician. The job of design is to hand them a clear plan, so nothing has to be guessed. A 3D render lets you see the light on the materials before anything is fixed in a wall. Coordinating the electrician, the carpenter and the suppliers in this way is a central part of renovation support and trade coordination.

Notes by room

Kitchen

Kitchen lighting starts at the worktop: a strip under the wall cabinets, hidden behind a lip or inside a profile. Above the island, pendants or recessed fittings. As a starting point, pendants usually hang about 75 to 90 cm above the worktop, adjusted to the height of the people seated and to the sightline across the space. This is how we plan the whole kitchen, from the island to the niche.

Living and dining

In the living room the ambient layer is secondary and mood is what matters: indirect light, a floor lamp, a lit niche or shelf. Avoid downlights directly above the heads of people on the sofa, and favor light that comes off the walls. Watching TV is easier on the eyes with soft light behind or beside the screen, so it doesn’t glare against a dark wall. Over the dining table, center the pendant on the table, not on the room. If the table’s position isn’t settled yet, leave room in the ceiling to move it.

Bathroom

Face light from the front or the sides, at eye level, to avoid shadows under the eyes. Around it, recessed ambient light in fittings suitable for a wet environment, as the electrician specifies. A low strip under a wall-hung vanity works as a gentle night light. More on the decisions that shape a bathroom.

Bedroom

Pendants on either side of the bed free up the side table and bring light down to reading height. Switches by the bed save getting up in the dark. Avoid a spot aimed straight at the pillow, and choose warm, low light that’s pleasant before sleep. The bedroom pictured is part of the dark-oak bedroom project.

Entrance and hallways

This is where the first impression of a home is set. A switch by the door that brings up soft light in the entrance, not the whole ceiling, gives a calm welcome to whoever comes home in the evening. Low light, for example a strip under the entrance cabinet on a motion sensor, lets you walk through at night without lighting up the whole house. In a hallway, light washing one wall is calmer than a row of downlights along the ceiling, which marks out the corridor like a landing strip.

Good lighting doesn’t draw attention to itself. You feel it rather than see it. Planned together with the materials, it’s the detail that ties everything else together.

Frequently asked questions

Which color temperature suits a home: 2700K or 3000K?

Both are warm and suit a home. 2700K is warmer and softer, and common in living rooms and bedrooms. 3000K is slightly crisper and comfortable in kitchens and bathrooms. In an open-plan space, keep to one temperature, or at most a small difference, such as 2700K in the living room and 3000K in the kitchen.

What is concealed lighting, and where does it work?

An LED strip hidden in a profile, a niche, under a shelf or behind a mirror, so you see the light and not the source. It works in kitchen niches, on mirrors, on stone and wood walls and as a hallway night light. It’s a mood layer, so it still needs ambient and task light beside it.

When should lighting be planned in a renovation?

Before the electrician starts marking points, and before the joinery and stone drawings go to fabrication. Channels for light strips, space for drivers and the depth of dropped ceilings are all set at that stage. Once the walls are closed, every change means opening them up again.

What is CRI, and why does it matter?

The color rendering index describes how true to life colors look under a light source. In a home with wood and natural stone, choose a CRI of 90 or above, so materials, food and skin tones look as they really are and don’t turn gray.

How high should pendants hang over a kitchen island?

A common starting point is about 75 to 90 cm above the worktop. The exact height depends on the size of the pendant, the ceiling height, and the sightlines of people seated at the island and of anyone looking across from the living room.
